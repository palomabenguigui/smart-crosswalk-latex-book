## Key findings (own words)
- Video observation at six signalised crosswalks in Hong Kong (urban, crash-prone, moderate volumes). 6,320 pedestrians
  arrived during red across 652 cycles, and 1,169 (18.5%) crossed on red. Mean cycle 151.8 s, mean pedestrian red 72.5 s (ms. p. 11).
- Random-parameter logit model (ms. pp. 13–14):
  - Women had lower odds than men (β = −0.405, OR 0.667). Older pedestrians had higher odds (OR 1.208), adolescents lower (OR 0.834).
  - Having a companion lowered the odds (β = −0.546, OR 0.579). **Each additional pedestrian nearby** lowered them too (β = −0.379, OR 0.685).
  - **Another pedestrian violating in the same cycle** raised the odds strongly (β = 1.392, OR 4.023).
  - **Longer red time (β = 0.061 per s) and longer time-to-green (β = 0.072 per s) raised the odds** (5% level).
    For our reading (not the authors'): e^0.061 ≈ 1.06, i.e. about 6% higher odds per extra second of red. Label it as derived.
  - More traffic and more heavy vehicles lowered the odds.
- Discussion (ms. p. 19): impatience grows with the anticipated wait. Hong Kong has **no** countdown heads, yet pedestrians still
  estimate the wait from their surroundings. The authors suggest that **pedestrian green should respond to pedestrian and vehicle volumes**,
  noting that reliable pedestrian-tracking technology is available. They leave the effect of a "countdown to green" open.

## Why it matters
- The strongest single source for the **adaptive-red** component: red length matters per second, a lone pedestrian is at higher risk
  (the companion and crowd effects replicate `rosenbloom2009` with a much larger sample), and the authors recommend demand-responsive timing.
- The imitation effect (OR 4.0) agrees with `lefkowitz1955` and `faria2010` and contrasts with `rosenbloom2009` p. 393. Use it in the ch. 4 matrix.
